# 02 — Story and Lore Bible

## 2.1 The cosmology

Four layers, and the game moves down through them.

**Ụwa** — the world of the living. Villages, farms, the ogilisi tree. The player never
sees a living village; the game begins after the protagonist has already left it. That
absence is the point.

**Ọhịa / Ajọ Ọfịa** — the bush, the evil forest. The buffer. In Igbo practice, the ajọ
ọfịa is where things that cannot be buried in the village go: twins, the diseased, the
ọgbanje. It is not hell. It is the municipal edge of the moral world.

**Ala Mmụọ** — the land of spirits. Where the dead go and where the alusi live. It is
not below in a physical sense; it is *adjacent*. Our rooms 6 through 9 are already
inside it, which is why the sky in room 9 is wrong in a way the player cannot name.

**The alusi themselves** — Ala, Amadioha, Idemili, Ikenga. Not layers but presences.
They are in the ground and the air and the water. They grant things to people who
interest them and they do not explain why.

Between Ụwa and Ala Mmụọ there is traffic, and the ọgbanje is that traffic.

## 2.2 Main plot

**Act One — The charm.**
A woman carries a child nine times. Nine times it dies before the yam comes up. On the
tenth, a dibia finds the iyi-uwa — the buried token that ties a spirit-child to the
world — and buries it under the ogilisi tree, which should end the cycle. The child
lives. It grows. It is meant to become ordinary.

Then something takes the mother, before the year turns. Something that walks at night
and answers to no name the dibia can find.

So the child goes back to the ogilisi tree and digs up its own charm, breaking the
bargain from the wrong side. It can no longer die properly. Neither, it will turn out,
can the thing that took her.

> **[CONFLICT — for Midas to settle.]** This section says nine burials and a tenth
> child. **The shipped game says nine children and eight burials**, consistently and in
> three separate places: `GRAVES` is eight mounds with the ninth grave being the hole
> the player wakes in (`GRAVE_LINES[7]`: *"There is no ninth mound. The ninth was dug
> up."*), Onwe's bestiary entry calls it *the ninth child*, and the nine strokes of nzu
> then read as one per grave rather than one per burial — which is the tidier reading,
> since the player is wearing their own. The game's version is internally coherent and
> `HANDBOOK.md` follows it. Either the prose above or the tables need to move; do not
> silently pick one while editing something else.

**Act Two — The name.**
The player descends: the path that watches, the shaft, the dark. They meet **Ogbunabali**,
he who kills at night, and discover they cannot hurt him. He heals faster than they cut.
He tells them, repeatedly, that he did not take her.

The answer is on a stone in the shaft, chalked in nzu: nine strokes and a name. Naming
him makes him mortal for seven seconds at a time. The player kills him. He was telling
the truth. He did not take her. He was paid to be seen doing it.

**Act Three — The one who was paid.**
The bone road, past the market and the water. **Ekwensu** — not the devil the
missionaries made of him, but the deity of war and bargaining, who takes contracts.
He does not need naming. He is not hiding. He took a fee and he will say what it was
if you can make him.

The fee, it turns out, was not paid in cowries.

**Act Four — Onwe.**
Fire, then open sky, then Ala Mmụọ. The last thing in the game is standing where the
mother should be. It is the player's own height. It holds its hands the way the player
holds theirs when pretending not to be afraid. It is **Onwe** — *self*.

The thing that was paid to take the mother was the child's own broken bargain. When you
dug up your iyi-uwa, something had to balance. A life was owed for a life kept. The
mother was not taken *from* you. She was taken *for* you, and the contract was in your
name, and you signed it the day you put your hand in that soil.

Onwe is what you were supposed to become — the tenth child, the one who went back.

**The ending.** You kill yourself, or you don't. See 2.7.

## 2.3 Timeline

Deliberately vague on years — Igbo oral chronology is generational, not dated.

| When | What |
|---|---|
| Long before | Ala settles the ground and the arrangement between Ụwa and Ala Mmụọ is made. Ọgbanje traffic begins. |
| Before living memory | Ogbunabali takes his office: he who kills at night. Not evil. A function. |
| Two generations back | The village is founded near the ogilisi tree. |
| The mother's youth | She is warned she carries an ọgbanje. She refuses to stop trying. |
| Nine deaths | Over roughly nine years. Each child buried without ceremony, as is the custom for ọgbanje, to discourage return. |
| The tenth birth | The dibia finds the iyi-uwa. Buries it under the ogilisi. The cycle breaks. The child lives. |
| ~10 years of ordinary life | **[NOT BUILT]** — a strong candidate for a playable prologue or a lore-room flashback. |
| The taking | The mother is taken in the night, before the year turns. |
| Three days later | The child digs up its own charm. **The game begins here.** |
| The game | Days? Hours? Time does not work correctly this far down and the game should never state a duration. |

## 2.4 The protagonist

**Name: none.** The player character is never named, and this is load-bearing — the
entire game is about names having power, so the one being who goes unnamed is the one
being who has not yet been fixed in place. Do not name them. Ever.

- Wears a bone-white mask. Nine strokes of nzu across the brow, one for each burial.
- A red sash. Cowrie beads at the throat.
- Does not speak in cutscenes except once, in the opening, when the voice profile
  switches to `you` for the line about digging up the charm.
- Is not a hero and is never described as one.
- Its verbs are all returns: it respawns, it comes back, it will not stay dead.

## 2.5 The gods and spirits

### Ala — the earth, and the most senior
Owner of the ground and of morality. Every crime is ultimately against her. Grants
**Ala's Fall** (the ground slam) and **Grasp of Ala** (roots enemies where they stand).
The ledger is called **Ala's Ledger** because she keeps accounts.
*Voice: never speaks. She is the floor.*

### Amadioha — lightning, justice, the open sky
Strikes the guilty. Grants the **Assent of Amadioha** — nine bolts on everything
standing. The most straightforwardly aggressive alusi and the first spell the player
gets.
*Voice: thunder only.*

### Idemili — water, the pillar
The python is hers. Grants the **Veil of Idemili** — water standing between you and the
next wound. Her room, Iyi Idemili, is the gentlest place in the game and the only one
where the music is in a major-adjacent mode.

### Ikenga — the right hand
Properly an *object*: a carved personal shrine with horns, made by a man to hold his own
achievement. Grants the **Hand of Ikenga** — your right hand answering to you alone,
briefly. That "briefly" is theologically correct and should never be made permanent.

### Ogbunabali — he who kills at night — **BOSS 1**
A real office, not an invention. He kills at night and only at night, and he does not
negotiate. Our version has been paid to be *seen*, which is a humiliation for him, and
his lies are defensive rather than malicious. He is closer to a bailiff than a demon.
Lines (already in the code): *"I did not take her." "You were never buried." "There is
no name under the chalk." "Your mother sent me." "Stop swinging. I am already gone."*
Every one of these is true in some sense except the fourth.

### Ekwensu — the one who was paid — **BOSS 2**
**Critical:** Ekwensu is *not* Satan. Missionary translation flattened him into the
Christian devil and that flattening is one of the things this game exists to push back
on. He is a deity of war, bargaining, and reversal — invoked before battle, honoured at
festival. Our Ekwensu is enormous, mercenary, and completely honest. He does not lie
because he has no reason to. He is the only boss who tells the player the truth
unprompted, and it is the worst thing they hear.

### Onwe — self — **FINAL BOSS**
Not a traditional figure. **[PROPOSED — this is our invention and should be flagged as
such if the game is ever presented as ethnographic.]** Onwe is the tenth child: the one
who accepted the arrangement and went back. It is what the player would have been. It
fights with the player's own moveset, which is a design commitment as much as a
narrative one — see `04-COMBAT.md`.

### The chalk masquerade — **TUTORIAL MINI-BOSS**
A masquerade of nzu that stands up in the clearing after the opening. It does not hate
you. It is a teacher — it drills you with slow, white-outlined swings and only goes live
at the end. It is the first thing in the game and the first thing that dies, and there
is something sad about that which the game never comments on.

## 2.7 Endings

**All three endings are implemented.** `G.ending` is 1, 2 or 3, saved, and named on the
stats card.

### Ending A — "Nkwụghachi" (the return) — **BUILT**
The player kills Onwe. The contract is void because one of the two parties is dead.
The mother is not returned — nothing returns her — but the debt is closed, and the
player can die properly now, someday, which is the only thing they actually wanted.
The stats card follows: deaths, kills, lore recovered, time.

Tone: not triumphant. Quiet. *"Now you can die properly, someday, which is all you
ever wanted and not at all what you came for."*

### Ending B — "Nlọghachi" (the going back) — **BUILT**
At the moment Onwe's guard breaks, do not press Z. Wait. The prompt fades. Onwe lowers
its hands. The player may walk into it.

The player accepts the arrangement: goes back, becomes the tenth child, the cycle
resumes, the mother is returned to the moment before the taking, and remembers none of
it. She will bury nine more children. The player chose that.

Tone: the cruellest ending and the most loving one.

*As built:* when Onwe's broken window **runs out without being spent**, it lowers its
hands for 420 frames and says so. Walk into it and you get ending 2. Stand there and the
window closes — it picks its hands back up, tells you that you spent the moment, and the
fight resumes. The offer is repeatable on the next break.

**An execution can never trigger it**, because `startExec`'s boss branch sets `broken`
to 0 outright and the decrement path is what opens the offer. That is implicit, so if
you ever change the execution to wind `broken` down rather than zero it, this needs a
guard — there is a REGRESSION test on it. An earlier `execUsed` flag was written for
this and deleted once mutation testing proved it never affected anything.

### Ending C — "Onye Ọma" (the one who did not) — **BUILT**
Reach Onwe having killed nothing that was avoidable — every optional enemy left alive.
Onwe cannot fight you, because it is you, and you did not. This one requires real
discipline and should be undiscoverable without the codex hinting at it.

*As built:* Onwe spawns with its hands already down and never raises them. Walk into it
for ending 3. The codex hint is the `ONWE` lore entry, which opens once you have seen
it and ends "a thing that can only copy has a problem if it is given nothing to copy" —
oblique enough not to instruct.

**`G.spared` is a flag, not a counter, and that is a deliberate deviation.** Enemies
respawn every time you enter a room, so a tally of spared creatures is not well defined
— you could farm it. A tally of *avoidable kills* is well defined, so `G.spared` starts
at 1 and drops to 0 the first time anything avoidable dies. What counts as avoidable is
read off the world by `bossIsGated()`: a boss some exit is gated behind had to die, and a
boss nothing is gated behind did not. Ikuku is optional, so killing it costs you this
ending. The chalk teacher comes apart whether you fight it or skip it, so it never
counts. Bosses are all gated and therefore unavoidable; the teacher comes apart whether you
fight it or skip it.

The stats card carries a `left standing` row so the run tells you which way it went.

### What all endings share
- No credits crawl. A stats card, the title, silence.
- The save is retained. There is no "you have completed the game" lock.
- ~~NG+ offer on the stats card~~ — **[BUILT]**. `N` on the ending card, under the
  `Z` prompt, and the card carries a **times round** row.

## 2.8 NPCs — the honest gap

**There are currently zero NPCs in this game.** Every entity is hostile or scenery.
This is the largest content gap and the biggest single opportunity. Full design in
`05-PROGRESSION-AND-NPCS.md`, but narratively, the four that should exist:

1. **The dibia who buried your charm** — in the night market, dead now, still working.
   He does not recognise you. He is the exposition character and the game's only warmth.
2. **A market woman selling nothing** — she has one line per visit, and over the course
   of the game she is describing your mother without knowing it.
3. **Another ọgbanje, younger, still in the cycle** — met in Iyi Idemili. Asks you what
   is on the other side of not going back. You cannot answer.
4. **Your mother's shade** — in Ala Mmụọ, before Onwe. She does not know you. This is
   the emotional peak of the game and it should be brief and unsentimental.

## 2.9 Dialogue style guide

Hard rules for every line of text in the game.

- **Short declaratives.** No subordinate clause pile-ups. *"He falls quiet."*
- **No exclamation marks.** Anywhere. Ever.
- **No archaic English.** No "thee," "'tis," "hark." The register is contemporary and
  flat.
- **Second person for the narrator, present or simple past.** *"You die again."*
- **Bosses speak in quotation marks; the narrator does not.** This is already the
  convention in the cutscene data and must be maintained.
- **Igbo words unglossed in dialogue, glossed in the codex.**
- **The narrator is dry and slightly wry, never portentous.** If a line could appear on
  a fantasy novel's back cover, rewrite it.
- **Cruelty is delivered flatly.** *"You were not supposed to remember the word."*
- **Maximum ~110 characters per cutscene beat** — beyond that the typewriter takes too
  long and the player skips.
- **Never explain a mechanic in the fiction's voice.** Tutorial prompts are a separate
  register and are allowed to say "press Z." Cutscenes never are.

### Examples — right and wrong

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| "Behold! The ancient evil stirs!" | "Something took her before the year turned." |
| "You feel a surge of power course through you." | "Something heavy settles into your open hand." |
| "The ọgbanje, a spirit child of Igbo belief, returns..." | "Nine times she buried you before the yam came up." |
| "Your mother loved you very much." | "She kept trying. That was the problem." |
